Your customer is not choosing between brands first. She is choosing between two fears: being seen grey, and what the colour will do to her skin. Today she trades one against the other. From the call, one way is to colour anyway and manage the reaction with antihistamines and creams. Others, we expect, switch to henna or stop colouring. Research will tell us which is most common. Why the brand matters here

Gentleness can’t be judged from a shelf

Search qualities

Judged before buying: the shade, the price, the pack size.

Experience qualities

Judged only after use: does it cover the greys, does it itch?

Credence qualities

Your promise sits in the last two columns. When a promise can’t be checked before buying, the brand is what she leans on.

A frame from our ISB brand equity session: brands matter most when people cannot check a product before buying it. Shade and price can be checked on the shelf. Whether it covers greys and whether it itches is only known after use. Long-term effects on the scalp are hard to judge even then. Your core promise lives in those last two. For someone who has reacted before, trying is a real risk. So the brand has to earn her trust before the first use. Everything that follows serves that one job.

Four beliefs shape how we work. One: a brand is a set of associations in the customer’s memory, not a logo; we were taught that brands are knowledge structures in memory. This is the communication model we use, from our ISB course. You are the sender. We encode your vision into a name, a story, a pack and claims. 1 · Encoding the message

Five kinds of noise stand in the way

01

Every pack says “safe”

Herbal, natural, ammonia-free, PPD-free. The idea has worn thin.

02

Your own caution label

Hair dyes carry a skin-test caution. Yours will too, right beside the promise.

03

Her memory

One bad reaction teaches her to doubt every promise after it.

04

The word “chemical”

Honest, and it sounds harsh. “Natural” feels safer, even when it isn’t.

05

A crowded marketplace

Endless listings, sponsored ads and AI-made creatives that look alike.

Noise is anything that distorts the message on its way to her. Five kinds matter here. The category already claims safety in many words, so the idea is worn. Your own pack will carry a skin-test caution, which sits right next to a promise of gentleness. Her memory of a past reaction makes her read every promise sceptically. Framing research we studied at ISB shows that words like natural feel positive and words like chemical feel negative, so the honest word works against you. And the marketplace is crowded. The brand has to be designed against each of these, not just look good.

1 · Encoding the message

One message, three ways to read it

The reading we want

“Finally, one I can try.” She does the skin test, sees nothing, and colours that weekend.

A negotiated reading

“Gentle, so it probably won’t cover my greys.”

An opposed reading

“Chemical? Then it’s like all the others.” Or: “For allergies, so not for me.”

After Stuart Hall’s encoding and decoding model. Our job is to make the first reading the likely one, and to test it with people who react before you sign off.

Stuart Hall showed that the same message can be read three ways: the way it was meant, a negotiated way that accepts part of it, and an opposed way that rejects it. For you, the danger readings are clear. Gentle can be heard as weak on greys. Chemical can be heard as harsh. A product for allergies can be heard as not for me. We will draft the brand against all three, then put the work in front of people who react and listen to what they actually take away.

In a UK taste test we studied, people preferred Pepsi when they could not see the label, and Coke by a wide margin when they could. The drinks did not change; the names did. That gap is brand equity: what the name adds to the product. On the right is how it builds, using the five steps of the brand value chain: awareness, associations, attitudes, attachment and activity. Hair colour is bought every few weeks, so attachment and activity matter a great deal: a customer who trusts you keeps coming back.

This is the network of associations we want in her memory, sorted the way the ISB model sorts them: attributes, benefits, emotions, plus the proof that makes them believable. Each one has to be strong, favourable and unique. Black lines are points of difference, which only you can own. Grey lines are points of parity: every good colour must match them, or she will not even consider you. Just as important is what we keep out. Medical makes her feel like a patient. Herbal is a claim you cannot make. Weak on greys and only for allergies shrink the brand.

Customers can’t, or won’t, always tell you why they buy. The purchase is driven by the motives she doesn’t say, so the research has to reach them.

In interviews people give manifest motives: coverage, no itching, ease, price. Those are true, but every brand answers them. The purchase is usually driven by latent motives, which people cannot or will not say out loud. Our hypotheses, from the call: she wants to look like herself, not older than she feels; she does not want to be the one in the family who cannot colour; she wants control over how she ages; and she wants to feel that someone made this for her. The research tests these and finds the ones we have missed.

3 · Identity and self

Colour is about how she sees herself

Her actual self

Who she is: the greys are showing, and her skin reacts.

Her ideal self

Who she wants to be: unbothered, put-together, colouring on her terms.

Her social self

Who others see: at the function, in the photos, at work.

Most people buy colour the self-expressive way: feel, do, then know. 
Fear has turned it into a careful, utilitarian buy: know, feel, then do. 
The brand should give her the first way back.

People buy to close the gap between who they are and who they want to be, and to manage how others see them. Hair colour sits right on that gap. Her actual self has greys and a reactive scalp. Her ideal self is unbothered and put-together. Her social self is what people see at the function. The ISB course describes three decision modes. Colour is normally a self-expressive buy, led by feeling. Fear has made it a careful, research-first buy for her. A strong brand lets her buy it with feeling again.

3 · Laddering

From ingredient to identity

In every interview we keep asking one question: why does that matter to you?

Attribute

Gentler chemistry

Functional

No itching, no swelling

Functional

She colours whenever she needs to

Psychosocial

Nobody sees her greys, or her struggle

Value

Self-respect: she stays herself

A means-end chain, written as an example of the method. The real ladders come from her answers, and the top rungs are where the brand speaks.

Laddering is the interview method we use to reach latent motives. You start from a product attribute and keep asking why it matters, until you reach a value. Here is an illustrative ladder: gentler chemistry means no itching, which means she can colour whenever she needs to, which means nobody sees her greys or her struggle, which comes down to self-respect. Competitors talk at the bottom rungs. The brand earns its place by speaking at the top ones, while the product proves the bottom ones.

Positions are our reading of the call and the shelf. The lead survey replaces them with your customers’ own ratings.

A perceptual map plots how customers see the options on the two things they care about most. From the call, those are how harsh or gentle a colour feels and how much colour it delivers. Today the options sit along a diagonal: mainstream creams give full colour but feel harsh; henna and herbal feel gentle but give limited colour; ammonia-free and natural claims sit in between. The top-right corner, full colour that feels gentle, is empty. That is the space your formula earns and the brand has to claim. The positions are our hypothesis; the survey of your leads will put real numbers behind them.

Relationship norms (Aggarwal, 2004): a friend is judged by a friend’s rules. If something goes wrong, the brand has to respond like one.

People form relationships with brands: best friends, flings, marriages, acquaintances. We choose the bond first and work backwards. A fling depends on surprise, which is the last thing she wants. An acquaintance is easy to replace. A doctor is trusted but distant, and makes her feel like a patient. The bond we propose is a friend who has been through it. Below are five is and is-not pairs that turn that into a voice. Research by Pankaj Aggarwal, who taught our ISB course, adds a warning: customers judge friend-like brands by friendship rules. So customer service and how you handle a reaction must behave like a friend, too.
